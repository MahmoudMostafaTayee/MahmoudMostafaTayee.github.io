Traffic analytics platform Use case · lateral velocity + conflict analysis

Most lane changes are ordinary. Finding the dangerous ones is the problem.

A lane change becomes an incident only when it is abrupt and creates a conflict — forcing another track to react. The evaluator measures lateral motion against lane geometry and nearby traffic, and stays silent on everything else.

01The problem

"Changed lanes" is a classification; "changed lanes dangerously" is a relationship.

Nearly every lane change on a monitored road is legal and unremarkable — signalling, decelerating, drifting behind a slow truck. A detector that fires per lane change trains operators to ignore it within a week. The distinguishing property of a dangerous lane change is not the change; it is the conflict it creates: sustained closing velocity toward another vehicle, a cut-in that forces a brake or a swerve.

That is inherently a two-track, time-extended judgment. One frame cannot carry it; even one track alone cannot.

Design target: alert only when abrupt lateral motion coincides with a measurable conflict — one alert per incident, evidence showing both vehicles.

02Lateral motion + conflict

  • Lane assignment over time. Each track is assigned to a lane per frame with hysteresis, so jitter between lanes doesn't manufacture phantom crossings. A lane change is an actual sustained transition: sustained movement from lane A to lane B across a window.
  • How abrupt. Lateral displacement rate, lateral velocity, and heading change are integrated over the transition. A slow merge never qualifies no matter how tight the lane geometry is.
  • The conflict test. At the moment of maximum lateral velocity, the evaluator looks at the receiving lane's occupant behind: closing speed, projected time-to-collision, and whether the cut-in forces a reaction (which shows up as the other vehicle's own velocity/heading change). No conflict — no alert, however abrupt. A conflict with no abruptness (traffic squeezing) is not this evaluator's brief.
  • Confirmation. Like every behaviour on the shared core, the event fires after the condition is sustained, with a cooldown that collapses bursts of duplicate reports into one incident.

04The hard parts

01

Metric ambiguity

Lateral velocity is only meaningful in scene units, not pixels — and pixel-to-scene scale varies wildly with camera distance. Thresholds were fitted per camera and per speed band: the same measured sway means different things on a highway approach and inside a yard.

02

Perception noise at the critical moment

The transition's worst cases: id switches, box flicker, occlusion by the receiving vehicle's own cut-in. The evaluator's armed window deliberately reads a couple seconds of buffered history, so the conflict rule is evaluated on the smoothed trajectory rather than on the frame where everything is moving fastest.

03

Honest scope

Lane changes that indirectly cause a rear conflict (slamming brakes) produce a different pattern: the conflict is behind, not beside. Rather than overloading one evaluator, the behaviour emits a reduced-confidence alert with the conflict type labelled — the evaluator knows where its definition has limits and says so in the event metadata.

05Reflection

Conflicts are relationships; measure them as such.

Building this evaluator shifted how I framed event definitions: not "what does the scene look like", but "what relationship between two tracks over time is dangerous". Every subsequent behaviour — falls included — got easier to specify once the primitives (trajectory, lane, occupancy, closure) existed.
