On-site analytics platform Use case · zones + dwell + attribution

Queue length and wait time, measured from the cameras that already watch the counter.

Waiting was counted by hand, per shift, at the counter. The system measures it from tracked trajectories: an entrance line opens a visit, a dwell timer turns loitering into waiting, seat stillness starts service — backdated to where it truly began — and every number survives camera handoffs.

01The problem

Operational metrics lead to disputed numbers the moment they must be computed by hand.

Hand-tallied queue lengths are a snapshot someone remembers, and wait-time targets were checked only after complaints. A camera-based version sounds simple and is not: people walk through the waiting area without waiting; they leave and come back; the counter and the waiting area are on different cameras; staff stand where customers stand. Every one of those cases manufactures false metrics if it is not modelled.

Design target: a wait is a duration defined by behaviour, not by detection boxes — and the same customer stays the same customer across cameras.

02What counts as waiting

  • Zones matched to anatomy, not pixels. The entrance is a floor line tested against the feet; waiting zones are polygons tested against the lower torso. The same polygons pair across cameras, so one visit survives a camera handoff unchanged.
  • Dwell, not presence. A wait only starts once a track has dwelt in the waiting zone for a threshold time, and it restarts if the person goes out of sight too long to still plausibly be in line. A person walking through contributes zero.
  • Gap tolerance. Short detector dropouts do not restart the clock; the stay state is keyed on the stable track ID and upgraded to a cross-camera global ID when one becomes available.
  • Targets snapshot at visit time. Each visit records which target policy was in force when it began — so today's policy edits can never reinterpret last week's reports.

03Service attribution

The subtlest problem was deciding when a wait ended. A person sitting down looks identical to a person waiting while seated, and the counter staff are indistinguishable from customers by appearance alone. Two mechanisms carry it:

  • Backdating by stillness. A still-in-seat dwell above the seat threshold latches the customer, and the service start is backdated to when the stillness began — the report reads the truth, not the first frame the box happened to be comfortable.
  • Staff-vote logic. Seats the staff occupy are excluded from customer attribution by a per-cam vote mechanism, and cross-camera claim logic guarantees one physical customer is never counted twice.

Alerts open when a running wait crosses its target (no operator waits for the customer to complain), close cleanly when the wait ends, and feed a live view of the floor state with per-camera data freshness shown — stale tiles are labelled stale rather than silently misread.

Reports compute wait distributions over the day — percentiles, not just averages, because a mean wait says nothing about the driver at the 95th who decides how the venue feels. Stats are saved and exportable, and all zones are editable at runtime through a REST zone-editor; configuration changes take effect without touching code.

05Reflection

Definitions, not detections, decided whether the numbers were trusted.

The interesting engineering was almost entirely semantic: what threshold turns standing into waiting, what gap rules out a re-entry, whose stillness starts the clock. Zone dwell measured with grace and debounce turned out to be the most reused primitive in the platform — the same timer that waits for a customer powers vehicle-dwell and compliance variants elsewhere.
