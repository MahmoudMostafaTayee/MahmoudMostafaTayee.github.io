Fall detection, wrong-way driving, unsafe lane changes, and multi-camera tracking. Different questions, same substrate: detections, trajectories, and enough temporal context to tell an event from a false alarm.

01The problem

A single frame can't tell a fall from sitting, a lane change from a swerve, or a wrong-way car from a turn.

Traffic and indoor safety share a failure mode: the interesting event is a change over time, but most detectors only see one frame. A person on the ground may be resting; a vehicle crossing a lane may be parking; a car pointing the wrong way may be turning. Alerting on the frame produces noise. Missing the transition produces nothing.

The cameras were also heterogeneous: different heights, angles, lenses, frame rates, and lighting. A rule tuned on one view did not transfer to another. And the compute budget was real — edge boxes for some sites, cloud for others.

Design target: one detection-and-tracking substrate, per-camera calibration, and behaviour-specific temporal evaluators that emit one evidence-backed event per real incident.

02The shared core

Every behaviour consumes the same primitives: tracked objects with stable per-camera IDs, their trajectories, and a geometric model of the scene. The differences live in the evaluator, not in the pipeline.

  • Detection and tracking: YOLO-family detectors for people and vehicles, with per-camera track IDs and short-term motion history.
  • Scene geometry: lane polygons, direction priors, zones, and a ground-plane approximation where the camera allows it.
  • Track state: position, velocity, heading, bounding-box aspect ratio, and keypoints where pose is available.
  • Event policy: confirmation windows, cooldowns, and one alert per incident — not one alert per frame.

That separation mattered for delivery: a new behaviour became a new evaluator over the same tracks, not a new pipeline with its own model, queue, and deployment.

03The four behaviours

01

Fall detection

Pose keypoints from YOLOv8-Pose and MediaPipe provide the body's geometry; the detector's box provides a cheap fallback when keypoints are missing. The evaluator combines torso angle, the change in bounding-box aspect ratio, vertical centroid velocity, and post-drop stillness. A state machine requires a transition and a sustained end state, so sitting down or crouching does not fire.

The output is one event per fall, with the clip window around the transition. The hard part was not detecting “horizontal”; it was distinguishing a fall from lying down, bending, and being occluded by furniture.

02

Wrong-way driving

Each lane carries a direction prior from calibration. A tracked vehicle's motion vector is compared against that prior, but only inside the lane polygon and only after sustained opposite motion across several frames. Near intersections, the evaluator tolerates turns and temporary misalignment; a single reversed vector is not an event.

The confirmation window was the main lever for precision. It also made the alert explainable: the evidence clip shows the vehicle entering the lane and continuing against the flow, not just a box at one instant.

03

Unsafe lane change

The evaluator assigns each track to a lane over time, then measures lateral displacement, lateral velocity, and heading change. A lane change becomes unsafe when it is abrupt and creates a conflict with another track — a short time-to-collision or a cut-in that forces a response. Normal lane changes are not alerts.

Thresholds are per camera and per speed band. The same lateral velocity means different things on a highway and in a yard, which is why calibration is part of the model, not a preprocessing detail.

04

Multi-camera tracking

Appearance embeddings maintain identity across cameras, with camera topology and transition windows pruning impossible hand-offs. The tracker handles occlusion and re-entry, and keeps a global ID stable when a vehicle disappears for a few frames and comes back.

This was the substrate for the other behaviours: a wrong-way event on one camera can be linked to the vehicle's previous and next sightings, so the operator sees a journey rather than three disconnected alerts.

04Architecture

The pipeline is deliberately boring: detect, track, calibrate, evaluate, publish. The interesting work is in the evaluators and the event policy.

Evaluators are independent and side-effect free with respect to the tracker: they read track history and emit events. That makes them testable with recorded tracks, without a GPU, and it keeps the event policy in one place.

The suite produced event-level outputs rather than per-frame detections: one alert per incident, with the clip and the track context attached. Temporal confirmation reduced the noisy alerts that make operators stop trusting a system. The shared core meant a new behaviour could be added without duplicating the detection, tracking, or deployment path.

The systems were evaluated on recorded and live camera feeds across different site geometries. The behaviours that depended most on calibration — wrong-way and unsafe lane change — improved most when thresholds were fitted per camera rather than globally.

06Reflection

Temporal context is the product. The model is just the sensor.

The most valuable engineering work was not a new architecture. It was deciding what counts as evidence, how long to wait before confirming, and when to stay silent. A false alert is a product failure even when the detector's mAP is good.

If I revisited this, I would invest earlier in a track-level evaluation set — recorded sequences with labelled events, not just labelled frames. That is the only way to tune a confirmation window honestly.
